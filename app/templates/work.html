Pay and unemployment, on separate axes

{{ shared[0] if shared }}–{{ shared[-1] if shared }}

Why these are three charts and not one. Putting real earnings and the unemployment rate on a single pair of axes means choosing two scales, and whoever chooses them decides whether the two lines appear to move together. Stacking the panels keeps the years aligned vertically while each measure keeps its own scale and its own unit, so the reader compares them without a rendering choice doing the arguing.

Unemployment and employment rates are from the Labour Force Survey, which counts people where they live rather than where their employer is registered. These three share one unit and one plausible range, so they belong on one pair of axes. The participation rate is the share of the 15+ population in the labour force; the employment rate is the share in work; unemployment is the share of the labour force, not of the population, which is why it can rise while employment does too.

The monthly gap exceeds the hourly gap in every published category, because women work fewer paid hours. The hourly figure is therefore the one that isolates the rate of pay; the monthly one also carries the difference in hours worked. Both are already adjusted for observable characteristics, so neither is the raw difference in average pay between women and men.
